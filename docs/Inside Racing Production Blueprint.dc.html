Inside Racing — production blueprint Draft 1 · for review

How Inside Racing gets produced

A read of the August 2026 edition, broken down by where every page's content comes from and how much of it has to be authored each month rather than generated. The output side of this is fact, taken from the edition itself. The input side is my best inference and is marked where it needs your confirmation.

1. The edition is a fixed skeleton

Fifty-six pages, in a stable order, with a stable set of section types. Nothing in the August edition suggests the structure is decided each month — the decisions are about content within slots. That means the production problem is a data problem, not a layout problem.

2. Forty-four of the fifty-six pages are one dataset, rendered three ways

Pages 3–4, 5–25 and 26–46 are not three bodies of content. They are three views of the same thing: the approved race program for the period ahead.

One record per race, carrying:

meeting date · venue and sponsor-prefixed name · track (course proper, synthetic, Heath) · race number, name and type · distance · prizemoney and, where applicable, the full placing breakdown · rating or age band · sex restriction · VOBIS Silver / Gold / Platinum flags · series heat membership · ballot and weight conditions · nomination and acceptance fees · entry, acceptance and riders deadlines · N/C and Q/C markers

From that single record set, each section is a different projection:

Pages 3–4
Deadlines
Group the deadline fields by the day the action falls due, split into Feature Race Entry, Entries, Acceptances and Riders. No new information — a re-sort of fields that already exist on each race.
Pages 5–25
Summary chart
Meetings down the side, thirteen class bands across the top, prizemoney and distance in the cells. A pivot: each race lands in a band by its rating or age condition.
Pages 26–46
Race programs
The same records printed in full, meeting by meeting, with conditions and deadline blocks.

Two consequences worth naming. First, any deadline is printed at least three times in an edition, so consistency is only guaranteed if all three come off one source. Second, the sections cover different horizons: the August chart runs out to meetings in late October, while the detailed programs stop in early October. The horizon per section is a production rule, not a monthly judgement, and needs to be written down.

4. Where the month actually goes

On this reading, the monthly effort divides into four kinds of work, and only one of them is genuinely writing:

Generated
Comes out of a system. The work is extracting and formatting it, not deciding it. Roughly 44 pages.
Computed
Derived from generated data by a fixed rule — deadline pages, contents page numbers. No judgement involved.
Editorial
Cover, industry notice, Stewards' Room. Written or selected each month. Three or four pages.
Static
Carried forward until a policy or a service changes. Should be a library, not a re-key.

If that split holds, the leverage is entirely in the first two categories. The question I need answered before designing the process further is how the 44 generated pages are produced today: exported and formatted, or transcribed.

5. The shape I'd propose

1
One edition dataset. Every input normalised into a single set of records for the edition's horizon, before any page is laid out.
2
Validation before layout. Missing acceptance times, races that fall in no class band, series heats with no matching race, deadlines that fall on a day the calendar says is closed. Caught once, centrally, rather than found at proofing.
3
One template per section. Deadlines, chart, programs, jumps, series, agents. Each reads the dataset and renders itself, so a late program change reflows every section at once.
4
A standing library for static pages. Policies, notices and service ads held as approved assets with review dates, dropped in rather than rebuilt.
5
Editorial as the only authored step. Cover, industry notice, Stewards' Room. Everything else assembles around them.
6
One proof, one sign-off. With the data validated upstream, proofing checks the edition reads correctly rather than re-checking every figure.

6. What I need from you to take this further

  1. The actual inputs. What arrives each month, in what format, from whom — ideally a copy of one month's set.
  2. How the 44 generated pages are made today. Exported and styled, or typed up?
  3. The tool the edition is laid out in, and whether that is fixed.
  4. The production calendar. When each input lands, when proofing happens, when it publishes.
  5. Who owns each section, and where the approvals sit.

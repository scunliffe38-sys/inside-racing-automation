Inside Racing — architecture and handover {{ statusLine }}

What has been built, and where it should live

Fifty-odd pages of the monthly edition now build themselves from a folder of files. This sets out the three layers that do it, where each one physically runs today, and the move that turns it from a working prototype into something the production team runs without help.

1. The shape of the system

Three layers, in one direction. Files land in a folder, parsers read and validate them, page components lay them out, and the edition is written out as a PDF. Nothing is transcribed between layers and no page number is typed anywhere.

Inputs
Fifteen files under inputs/, stable filenames, overwritten each month.
Parsers
Thirteen modules that read, validate and reshape. One per section, plus shared readers.
Pages
Section components that pack columns until they run out of room, then report their extent.
Output
One PDF of the whole edition, plus a list of everything the parsers thought was doubtful.

2. Inputs

Every input has a fixed name and a fixed home. The production team overwrites a file and the section that reads it changes; nothing else does. Two arrive in shapes worth noting: the Victorian calendar comes as one workbook per month and is read as a continuous run in date order, and the cover photograph arrives at full camera resolution and is reduced to a working derivative before any page loads it.

3. Processing

Two shared readers sit underneath everything: one for workbooks, one for delimited exports, which handles the UTF-16 tab-separated files the reporting tool produces without a conversion step. Above them, one module per section, each of which validates as it reads and names the file and row number of anything doubtful.

Module Responsibility
xlsx.js, tsv.jsRead a workbook or a delimited export into rows. Encoding, separators and column positions are handled here so no section has to care.
settings.jsThe edition’s furniture, and the riders’ agents register. Checks the required settings are present and the date format is right.
pagination.jsThe printed running order. Adds up what sits in front of a section to get its first folio, drops conditional sections, and grows the advertising to reach a multiple of four.
ads.jsThe house-ad library and where each may run: a panel in unused space, a filler under a short page, a whole page before the back cover.
chart.jsFinds the monthly calendars, sorts them chronologically, warns on a gap, and pivots the meetings into the thirteen class bands.
deadlines.js, programs.jsThe two other projections of the same race record set — deadlines regrouped by the day they fall due, and the full conditions meeting by meeting.
permits.js, trials.js, jumpouts.js, series.js, notices.jsOne each for the register extracts, the trials and jump-out schedules, the series heats and the three written sections.

Pagination is an output

Sections that flow — deadlines, the chart, race programs, agents, the written sections — can only be counted by laying them out. Each reports the number of pages it produced, and every other page picks that up. Fixed sections declare their extent. Conditional sections take no pages when their switch is off and everything after them moves up. Nobody types a folio, and any page number left in the settings workbook is flagged as stale.

4. Outputs

One file that matters and one that should. Inside Racing Edition assembles every section into a single document and exports the whole edition in one pass — every input resolves before anything commits, the three flowing sections measure together, and off-screen pages skip layout. The edition settles in under four seconds. Three part-files remain as a fallback if a future month grows the extent past what one pass can hold.

The second output is the validation. Every parser already reports what it found doubtful — a missing expiry date, a row shifted a column, a contact number in an unusual form, a gap in the calendar sequence — but it reports to the browser console, where it is only seen by someone who knows to look. Turning that into a written report the producer reads is the single largest gap between what exists and what can be handed over.

5. Where all of this runs today

In one place: this project, in a browser, with me in the loop.

Files
Uploaded here by hand each time an input changes.
Code
Parsers and page components live in the project. There is no version history beyond the project itself, and no review step before a change takes effect.
Build
Someone opens the edition page and waits for it to settle.
PDF
Printed from the browser by hand.
Checks
Read from the browser console by someone who knows to open it.
Archive
None. Last month’s inputs are overwritten by this month’s.

That is the right arrangement for building the thing and the wrong one for running it. Four limits matter. The production team cannot start a month without me. A mistake in the code is discovered in the output rather than in review. There is no record of what September was built from once October overwrites it. And the one step that most needs to be mechanical — reading the validation before signing off a proof — is the step most easily skipped.

6. Recommended: code in GitHub, the month in OneDrive

Split the two things that change at different rates. The parsers, page templates, house ads and fonts change a few times a year and need review and history, so they belong in a repository. The month’s files change every month and belong where the production team already works. A small runner joins them, and the producer never sees the repository at all.

GitHub — the system
Parsers, page components, the pagination spine, the house-ad library, licensed fonts, and the standing artwork. Changes are reviewed before they ship and every edition can be traced to the exact version that produced it.
OneDrive — the month
One folder per edition, holding that month’s inputs and its outputs. September stays intact when October is built, so an edition can always be reproduced or a query answered after the fact.

A month, from the producer’s side

Copy this month’s files into Inside Racing / 2026-09 / inputs. Double-click Build Edition. Read the check report it writes beside the PDF. Open the PDF. None of that requires knowing what a parser is.

It is a loop, not a single event. Inputs are stable by the 10th of the preceding month and the first draft is produced that day; corrections are then made to the inputs and the build re-run until the producer passes it. So drafts never overwrite each other — the run takes the next number itself — and each draft keeps a copy of the inputs that produced it. That costs nothing, fifteen small files, and it earns the report its most useful line: what changed since the last draft. At draft two the producer is not asking whether the edition is right, but whether their corrections landed and nothing else moved.

The production team has no GitHub account, so the producer’s machine never authenticates to anything. Code is published from the repository into the OneDrive folder when a change ships, and the runner uses the copy it finds there. GitHub stays the place changes are reviewed and versioned; OneDrive holds the current published copy alongside the months.

What the runner does, in order: takes the published code, reduces the cover photograph to its working derivative, loads the edition against the month’s folder, waits for the flowing sections to settle, writes the PDF, and collects every warning the parsers raised into a plain report naming the file and the row. A failed input stops the run and says which file and which row, rather than printing a page with a hole in it.

Five things in the code have to change to get there, and none is large. Input paths are currently fixed to a folder inside the project and need to point at the month’s folder. Warnings need collecting into a report rather than going to the console. The runner needs to drive a single-pass build, which the whole-edition file already does. Cover derivative generation needs to move out of my hands and into the run. And the licensed fonts need to sit in the repository so every machine renders identically — until they do, bold copy is synthesised by the browser and the column packing cannot be trusted to the millimetre.

7. Getting there

First
Local runner
A folder and a shortcut, run by one person on one machine. Proves the build works unattended and produces the check report. Nothing about it is thrown away later.
Then
Code to GitHub
The same runner, now working from a published copy rather than whatever happens to be on that machine. Changes are reviewed before they ship, fixes reach everyone without touching anybody’s PC, and each edition is tied to a known version.
Later, if wanted
Hosted
Upload the files to a page, press Build, download the PDF. Nothing installed anywhere. This needs an owner inside IT and should only be started once the process has run a few months unattended.

The producer’s experience is identical at each step, which is the point: the move from the first to the second costs them nothing.

8. Testing

Two tests, doing different jobs. One proves the system is right before it is handed over. The other proves a month is right before it goes out, and runs forever.

Build and compare, against two known editions

August and September 2026 have both been produced by hand. Rebuilding each from the files that made it, and comparing against the published PDF, is a genuine regression test: the answer is already known. Two editions rather than one matters, because a single month can be matched by accident.

Comparison is not eyeballing two PDFs. Four things, in order:

•
Extent. Total pages, and the length of every section.
•
Folios. Every section starting on the page the published contents says it does.
•
Breaks. The flowing sections turning the page at the same meetings. This is the sensitive one: a fractional difference in leading moves a break, and a moved break moves everything after it.
•
Content. Counts first — meetings, races, agents, permits — then the first and last row of each section, then a sample through the middle.

Accepted differences are written down

Some differences are known and deliberate. The published edition carries a permits row whose wins do not add up; the race programs export mixes in jump-outs and trials. Each accepted difference is recorded once, with the reason, so it is not re-argued every time the test runs. Anything not on that list is a failure until it is explained.

After handover the test changes shape, because from October on there is nothing to compare against. A month passes when the check report is clean, the counts match what the producer expected to see, and the producer passes the output. That last step is not a formality and does not go away: automation moves where the judgement happens, it does not remove the need for it. The producer knows the content better than any validation rule, which is why the report leads with counts they can judge rather than with technical detail they cannot.

10. Still to build

Separate from the handover, and small against what is done.

•
The build review step. The run has to end by telling the producer what it thought was wrong with the inputs. Two kinds. Missing or malformed is already detected by the parsers — blank required fields, a row shifted a column, a date in the wrong format, a gap in the calendar sequence — and only needs surfacing in the report instead of the console. Abnormal is new, and is the more valuable of the two: a value that is present and well-formed but wrong. A prizemoney figure an order of magnitude out, a venue that has never appeared before, forty per cent fewer meetings than last month, an agent with twice the usual riders. That needs a baseline to compare against, which the per-month folders provide once a few editions have run. Until then it compares against the two rebuilt editions.
•
Jumps Racing Program. The page is reserved and the folios after it are correct, but it has no input and no layout. Needs the program, the jumps trials and the prizemoney breakdown.
•
Picnic Racing. Same position — reserved by its switch, not yet built.
•
Back cover. Standing artwork, not yet placed.
•
Race Programs runs one page long. Twenty-two pages against the published twenty-one, printing 124 meetings against 122. The export is a full year and mixes in jump-outs and trials, which are excluded by having no prizemoney; the remaining two meetings need chasing.
•
Licensed Gotham bold cuts. Bold copy across the programs, the chart and three page titles is browser-synthesised. Real bold will shift the column packing, so the fit needs re-checking once supplied.
